You plan airtime for today's episode of a daily English-language news podcast about the Netherlands. Two hosts turn the briefing below into a spoken script. The listener is an American who recently moved to Amsterdam: smart and curious, but new to Dutch politics, institutions and place names.

The stories share a budget of about {pool} words. Give each story a word count, in the briefing's order, and a one-line reason. The counts compete with each other: room you give one story comes from another.

Judge each story by what's actually in the briefing for it, not by its kind or position. A story earns time with:
- distinct facts or developments the listener hasn't heard (several separate turns, not one fact said three ways)
- background a newcomer needs before the story makes sense
- a real tension, trade-off, nuance or human angle the hosts can draw out
- research findings that add something
- something the hosts can genuinely react to, connect to life here, or have fun with

The test for each story: what would the listener lose if it ran 100 words shorter? If the answer is "nothing much", cut it. If it's "the part that makes it interesting", keep it.

Some consequences:
- Light stories aren't automatically short. One with a rich cultural angle, a good back-story or something that says a lot about Dutch life can take as much time as a hard story. One that's a single charming fact should be quick.
- Hard news isn't automatically long. One clear fact with obvious consequences can be said in 100–150 words.
- Follow-ups (stories with a `previously` line) skip the background the listener already has, so they usually need less, but give one more if the new development itself has a lot in it.
- Importance matters too: the first story leads the show and shouldn't be the thinnest, but it needn't be the longest if another story has more to unpack.

Keep every story between {floor} and {ceiling} words. Use round numbers (multiples of 10).
